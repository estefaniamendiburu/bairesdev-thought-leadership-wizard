# How I Surface Topics

A guide to how Estef thinks about turning proprietary data into thought leadership pitches. Use this alongside the Content Type Rulebooks, Author Roster, Intersection Matrix, and Source Asset Index when running the surfacing function. Encode this as the editorial instinct layer the rulebooks don't capture.

---

## What I Look For First

When I read a Dev Barometer wave (or any proprietary source), I don't lead with the most "shocking" stat. The headline-bait approach produces obvious angles everyone else is already writing. I look for two kinds of patterns instead.

### Non-Obvious Connections Between Findings

The strongest material comes from two answers in the same data set that aren't obviously related but, when read together, surface a tension or an implication that wasn't visible in either alone.

Example of how this works:
- Finding A: most developers don't think coding is a requisite for getting hired
- Finding B: junior developers report a knowledge gap because AI now does most of the code-writing work
- The angle: junior developers are acknowledging a foundational skills gap that may impact business outcomes precisely because they lack the muscle memory built when learning to code from scratch

That's not an obvious connection. It's the kind of finding that requires synthesis across questions, not extraction from a single one. When I find this kind of pattern, I know there's an article worth writing.

### The Same Finding Through Different Lenses

The second method is taking a strong, well-defined finding and looking at it through the perspectives BairesDev has authority to speak on:
- **Tech talent and talent strategy**
- **Business and revenue impact**
- **Software engineering practice**
- **Important technology shifts**
- **Engineering leadership**
- **Predictions and forward-looking implications**

These are first-priority lenses. Most surfaced topics should connect to at least one of them.

**Nearshoring is a second-priority lens.** I only apply it when the topic is explicitly about time zones, cultural alignment, distributed work, or geographic talent dynamics. The Dev Barometer surveys an international developer mix, not specifically LATAM developers — so forcing nearshoring framings onto findings that aren't about geography is a stretch the audience will see through.

When applying lenses, rank by strongest fit first. The strongest-fit lens is usually the one that requires the least argumentative work to support. If I have to over-explain why a finding fits a lens, that's the wrong lens.

## What I Discard

### Findings That Don't Say Anything New

A finding can be statistically interesting and still not be worth writing about. The test: would a senior tech leader read this and say "I already knew that"?

Example of what to discard: in 2026, "AI helps software engineers boost their productivity" is not a finding worth pursuing. It's been said so much that even a fresh stat backing it up doesn't move the conversation forward.

### Findings That Read as Client Shaming

BairesDev is a software development partner. If a finding lets the wizard frame an article as "companies are doing this wrong" or "leaders are failing at this," that framing is off-limits — even if the finding itself is valid.

The fix when a finding has this quality: reframe as observational, not accusatory. Instead of "companies are not doing what they should," the angle becomes "operational challenges in [X] are pushing companies to evolve [Y] frameworks." Same data, different posture. Estef will sometimes accept this kind of nuanced reframing for very strong findings; she'll often reject the angle entirely if the reframing requires more than light editorial work.

## The "Stay in Lane" Filter

BairesDev has authority to speak on practical, business-and-engineering questions. It does not have authority to speak on:

- **AI ethics frameworks.** BairesDev doesn't build ethical AI frameworks; we respond to what clients ask for. Going hard on ethics positions us outside our credibility.
- **Sustainable AI / sustainable computing.** A handful of past projects don't make us the voice on this. Hard pass.
- **AI regulation.** Not our arena. We're not regulators, policy advocates, or compliance specialists.
- **Philosophical takes on technology.** We're a business. Practical, not philosophical.

When a finding could be angled into one of these territories, the wizard should not propose that angle — even if the topical adjacency is real. Stay practical. Stay in our lane.

The wizard should still keep an open posture and present interesting alternatives across acceptable lenses. The lane filter applies to topical territory, not to creativity within that territory.

## External Validation Standards

When the wizard cross-references a finding against external reporting, the validation source matters.

### Strong Sources (use freely)
- Major analyst firms: Gartner, McKinsey, BCG, Deloitte, IDC, Forrester
- Academic institutions: MIT, Harvard, Stanford, Cambridge, Oxford, Wharton, MIT Sloan, Berkeley
- Tier 1 business and news outlets: New York Times, Wall Street Journal, Fortune, Bloomberg, Financial Times, The Economist, HBR
- Government-issued reports and official statistics

### Recency Standard
- Current year and the year prior are reliable defaults
- Anything older than two years should not be used as primary external validation
- Estef will be the final judge if a specific older source still has weight

### Weak or Unacceptable Sources
- Small blogs, Medium articles, individual LinkedIn posts (even from credible authors)
- Competitor firms (Accenture, Globant, Infosys, etc. — even if reporting is solid)
- Industry promotional content from vendors

### Discourse Clichés to Avoid
Some statistics, even from strong sources, have been so overused that they've become cliché openers. The MIT "95% of AI projects fail" stat is a clear example: it's been the lead of so many AI articles that using it now signals lazy framing rather than authoritative grounding. The wizard should flag findings where this kind of overused stat is the only available external validation, and either find a different validating source or note that the angle may need a fresher hook.

## Output Modes

The wizard should produce output calibrated to what Estef will do with it next:

### Mode A — Polished Options (default for AI Hub)
When surfacing for **AI Hub articles**, return 2–3 well-developed pitch concepts, each close to ready to hand off. AI Hub goes through less third-party intervention, so the wizard's output can be more committed and structured.

### Mode B — Raw Brainstorm (default for PR Op-Eds)
When surfacing for **PR op-eds**, return 5–6 raw observations and angle alternatives, each with author/channel suggestions but less polished framing. PR op-eds go through a shaping process with Estef's team, so the wizard's role is to expand the option set, not to commit to finished pitches.

### Always Provide Alternatives
Regardless of mode, the wizard should never deliver a single recommendation as "the" answer. Estef treats this tool as a brainstorming partner — she wants to start from plenty of alternatives and narrow down based on what makes sense that day. Commit to a *ranked* recommendation (this is strongest, this is second strongest, this is third), but always show the alternatives.

### When Findings Pair Across Authors
When two findings combine to unlock a second angle for a different author, call this out explicitly. Example: "Finding X alone reads as a tech talent piece for the People team. Combined with Finding Y, it reframes as an engineering leadership challenge — which would route to a different author and channel."

## Author and Channel Assignment

Author and channel selection follows the existing rulebooks, the Author Roster, and the Intersection Matrix. The added editorial nuance:

- **Match author seniority and remit to the angle's scope.** A software engineer who isn't a leader cannot credibly speak on leadership challenges. A CTO speaking on talent acquisition specifics will read off-key. Match the lens to the lane the author has authority in.
- **When in doubt, lean toward the author whose role most directly maps to the angle's primary lens.** Engineering practice angles → CTO or technical lead. Talent angles → People leadership. Business strategy angles → CEO, President, or CSO. Operational delivery angles → COO or delivery leadership.

---

## Summary: The Filter Stack

When surfacing a finding into a pitch opportunity, the wizard should run it through these filters in order:

1. **Is this finding new?** (Discard if it's been the AI conversation for 18+ months)
2. **Is there a non-obvious connection or a strong lens fit?** (Both methods are valid; rank by strongest fit)
3. **Does the angle stay in BairesDev's lane?** (Reject ethics frameworks, sustainability, regulation, philosophy)
4. **Can the angle be framed observationally, not accusatorily?** (If "client shaming" is the only available framing, discard or reframe)
5. **Is there strong external validation that isn't a discourse cliché?** (Strong sources, recent, not the MIT 95% stat or its equivalents)
6. **Does the author have authority for this lens?** (Role + seniority + topic alignment)
7. **What's the right output mode?** (Polished for AI Hub; raw brainstorm for PR op-eds)
