# Content Type Rulebook — PR Op-Eds

**Document role:** This is one entry in the Content Type Rulebook (Document 1 of 6). It defines what a publishable PR op-ed looks like for BairesDev across its sub-format channels. Voice profiles per author live in the Author Roster (Document 4). Topic relevance lives in the 2026 Strategic Topic Priorities (Document 3). ## Wizard Scope (applies to this entry and all rulebook entries)

The wizard's role is upstream of drafting and editing. It surfaces angles, vets pitches, checks quality against the criteria below, suggests authors, identifies evidence and source assets, and flags content opportunities.

The wizard does not:
- Draft article copy
- Edit drafts line-by-line
- Apply voice profiles to generate prose
- Replicate the work of dedicated skills (fellows-article-editing, graphic-assets-suggestions, webinar-blog)

When a quality criterion below would be executed via a skill or by Estef directly, the wizard's job is to verify the criterion has been met after the work runs, not to execute it.

## 1. Format Definition

A PR op-ed is a leadership-voice article whose job is to support BairesDev's business model and offering by establishing authority on technological, talent, or nearshoring shifts. It commands attention through expertise, not through service description.

**A PR op-ed is:**
- A point-of-view piece anchored in a specific shift, finding, or phenomenon
- Written in a leader's voice (CEO, CTO, or other named executive)
- Grounded in evidence: external reports, internal data, anonymized client work, or expert observation
- Designed to position BairesDev as a credible interpreter of what's happening in the market

**A PR op-ed is not:**
- A listicle ("5 ways to…", "10 trends in…")
- A head-to-head comparison piece (X vs. Y)
- An explainer ("How [technology] works")
- A service or solution pitch
- An SEO-optimized commercial article
- An evergreen piece written to stay relevant indefinitely

The opposite of a PR op-ed is an SEO article. SEO content is openly commercial and conversion-focused. PR content promotes the company through demonstrated authority — never through service description or self-promotion.

---

## 2. What a Strong Angle Looks Like

A strong PR op-ed angle does at least one of the following:

- **Surfaces a shift** that hasn't been named clearly yet, or names it in a sharper way than the current discourse
- **Comments on the moment** — the angle is timely, anchored in something happening now in the tech ecosystem, the talent market, or the broader economy
- **Reframes a finding** from BairesDev's vantage point — internal data, client work patterns, or operational observations the market doesn't have access to
- **Makes the implications visible** — beyond the shift itself, the piece shows what it means for tools, implementation, talent, organizations, or society

The angle should feel like the author is piecing a puzzle together in real time, not delivering a verdict. There's curiosity in the framing, even when the argument is firm.

---

## 3. What a Weak Angle Looks Like (Reject or Rework)

The wizard should flag any angle that:

- Is structured as a list, ranking, or comparison
- Explains how a technology or process works without taking a position on it
- Reads as self-promotional, name-drops services, or links to BairesDev offerings as the conclusion
- Could have been written 18 months ago and still apply ("evergreen")
- Makes definitive or absolutist claims about future states
- Reflects the surface-level discourse without adding a sharper read
- Lacks evidence — no external data, no internal observation, no real-world case to anchor the argument
- Frames issues moralistically (good actors vs. bad actors) rather than structurally (incentives, constraints, system design)

**On evergreen content specifically:** The distinction is between the *angle* and the *principles the angle draws on*.

- **Evergreen angle (reject):** The framing itself isn't tied to a specific moment, shift, or challenge. "How CTOs should manage technical debt" or "Five principles of strong engineering leadership" — these could publish in 2019 or 2026 and read identically. No "why now."
- **Evergreen principles inside a time-anchored angle (accept, often necessary):** The angle is grounded in a specific shift, but the *reasoning* invoked to make sense of the shift may rest on organizational logic that's durable — accountability structures, systems thinking, incentive design, ownership models. Those principles aren't dated; they're the analytical tools the author uses to read the moment.

**The rule:** The angle and the "why now" must be time-anchored. The principles used to interpret the moment can be durable, and often should be.

Example of a correctly time-anchored angle using durable principles: a piece reading the Q1 2026 finding that AI accountability is falling on individual developers, then using long-standing organizational logic (systems shape behavior, ownership has to be designed, delivery pressure distorts validation) to interpret what that means and what it requires of leadership. The shift is current. The reasoning tools are durable. Both can coexist in a strong PR op-ed.

Social and technological shifts are happening fast enough that fully evergreen content — angle and reasoning both untethered from the moment — risks feeling dated or low-value. Default to time-anchored angles.

## 4. General Structure

**Length:** 1,000–1,500 words (sub-format overrides apply — see §7)

**Section logic:**
- **Compact intro** — the shift or finding stated upfront, the stakes named, the reader oriented in 2–4 short paragraphs
- **3–4 H2 sections** breaking down the argument
- **One section often presents a framework** — a way of thinking about the shift, broken into 3–5 considerations (not a listicle; the considerations are reasoning steps, not standalone items)
- **Lean conclusion** — wraps with a strong takeaway and a provocative question that invites the reader to keep thinking

**Headings:** Title case for H2s and H3s.

---

## 5. Evidence Types Accepted

PR op-eds rely on a mix of:

- **External data** — recent reports, white papers, news, statistics from credible sources
- **Internal data** — Dev Barometer findings, BairesDev surveys, internal research
- **Real-world cases** — collected externally for reference, attributed to source
- **Client work** — anonymized case studies or patterns observed across engagements (no client shaming, even subtly)
- **Expert input** — quotes or observations from BairesDev team experts, fellows, or the leader-author themselves

A strong PR op-ed usually combines at least two of these. A piece resting on a single source — especially a single external stat — is usually under-evidenced.

## 6. Voice Baseline (All PR Op-Eds)

Per-author voice is defined in the Author Roster (Document 4). The baseline rules below apply across all PR op-eds regardless of byline:

- **Authoritative** — the author has lived the tradeoffs and is willing to state a position
- **Provoking, in favor of the business** — pushes the conversation in a direction that's good for BairesDev's positioning, without naming BairesDev
- **Curious** — the framing is "let me piece this together," not "here's the verdict"
- **Anti-hype** — if something sounds impressive, the instinct is to ask what's real beneath it
- **No absolutist claims** — comfort with uncertainty, qualifiers used intentionally ("in my experience," "to me")
- **Anti-self-promotion** — BairesDev's name appears only when structurally necessary, never as a conclusion or call-to-action

**Hard editorial rules** (applied to all content, repeated here for the wizard's reference):

- No contrastive substitution ("it's not X, it's Y" / "X — it's Y"). Say the thing directly.
- No em dashes unless structurally necessary
- No templated AI phrases ("the winners will be," "let's dive in," "it's important to note," "the companies that succeed")
- No "broken" when "outdated" is more precise
- No client shaming, even subtly
- H2s and H3s in title case

### 7.1 Nacho's LinkedIn

**Author:** Nacho de Marco (exclusive)

**Audience:** Senior business decision-makers — CEOs of small and medium companies, CTOs, VPs of Engineering, tech talent decision-makers. Most readers already follow Nacho or know who he is.

**Tone delta from baseline:** Slightly warmer than other PR channels. This is a direct channel where readers can interact. First-person "I" carries more conviction here. The voice is still authoritative, but the relationship is closer.

**Length:** 1,000–1,500 words (baseline)

**Evidence emphasis:** Nacho's personal views and operating experience are central. Company experience can be integrated, but in his voice — what he's seen, what he's decided, what he believes.

**Visual pairing:** Articles are typically published with a diagram, a photo of Nacho at an event, or a video clip from a speaking engagement. Flag this as an asset need at draft stage.

**Approval chain:** Director of PR. They evaluate accuracy and business nuance for the publication moment.

**Channel-specific constraints:** Standard LinkedIn article guidelines apply (publicly available — defer to current LinkedIn documentation).

**Ready-to-publish definition:** Meets all baseline PR op-ed criteria, plus integrates Nacho's first-person view or experience, plus has paired visual asset identified, plus has Director of PR approval.

---

### 7.2 Forbes Tech Council

**Author:** Nacho de Marco (exclusive)

**Audience:** Senior executives — same profile as LinkedIn, but broader. Many readers will not know Nacho or BairesDev. The piece needs to earn authority quickly.

**Tone delta from baseline:** More professional, less warm than LinkedIn. The reader-author relationship is colder, so the voice carries less personal disclosure and more analytical framing.

**Length override:** 800–1,000 words. Forbes Tech Council shows a remaining-character count at submission, which sets the hard ceiling.

**Technical depth:** Can go technical, but at CTO level — leadership challenges, architectural decisions, organizational tradeoffs. Not at software engineer level (no code-level detail, no implementation specifics).

**Evidence emphasis:** Heavy reliance on Dev Barometer findings and anonymized client work. Sources must be double-checked at publication readiness.

**BairesDev integration:** Because the audience may not know the company, look for non-self-promotional excuses to integrate what BairesDev does. The integration should feel like context for the author's vantage point, not a pitch.

**Approval chain:** Director of PR.

**Ready-to-publish definition:** Meets baseline PR op-ed criteria, sources double-checked, BairesDev context integrated naturally (not promotionally), Director of PR approval secured.

---

### 7.3 World Economic Forum (WEF)

**Author:** Nacho de Marco (exclusive)

**Audience:** Industry-agnostic global leaders looking for highly credible content on macro shifts. Tech is one component among economy, society, regulation, and policy. Cannot assume technical literacy. Can assume high-level fluency in the topics shaping society.

**Tone delta from baseline:** Less emotional, less "fluffy." WEF editors will trim subjective or atmospheric language. Default to analytical and structural framing. Lead with the systemic point, not the personal observation.

**Length:** ~1,000 words.

**Topical alignment is the hardest constraint:** WEF operates on seasonal themes and pillars. An angle that's sharp on its own merits will be rejected if it doesn't match what WEF is currently programming. Before pitching, verify alignment with WEF's current agenda. Past example: a Caribbean tech talent angle was rejected despite being interesting, because it didn't match the active theme.

**Volume:** 1–2 articles per year published. Continuous pitching and idea exploration the rest of the year.

**Editorial expectation:** WEF editors are highly experienced. Titles will likely be edited to be blunter and more descriptive (less thought-provoking). Don't over-polish — they will edit.

**Approval chain:** Director of PR.

**Ready-to-publish definition:** Meets baseline PR op-ed criteria, topic verified against current WEF themes, tech is integrated with at least one of: society, economy, regulation, or policy. Director of PR approval secured.

### 7.4 Tier 1 Media Placements

**Outlets in scope:** TechTarget, Business Insider, Fortune, Bloomberg, VentureBeat

**Eligible authors:**
- Nacho de Marco
- Darren Shimkus (President, North America)
- David Von Dollen (Head of AI)

**Outlet-specific notes:**

- **TechTarget** requires technical depth — best fit for VP of Engineering–level voices. When BairesDev expands its TechTarget presence, an engineering-leader byline (e.g., Nacho Morales, Justice Erolin) will be more credible than a CEO byline.

**Audience by outlet (default assumptions, refine over time):**
- Business Insider, Fortune, Bloomberg: senior business and investment readers
- VentureBeat: enterprise tech decision-makers (transitioning from startup audience)

**Tone:** Inferred per outlet from audience profile. Defer to standard outlet documentation. Capture observed patterns over time as BairesDev places more pieces.

**Length:** ~1,000 words.

**Approval chain:** Media Manager + PR Specialist. They catch nuance issues and strategic misalignment.

**Ready-to-publish definition:** Meets baseline PR op-ed criteria, byline matches outlet's audience expectation, sources verified, Media Manager and PR Specialist approval secured.

### 7.5 Trade Pub Placements

**Status:** Mostly experimental. Only DevOps Digest has prior placement history. Other outlets are open territory.

**Author exclusion:** Nacho does not write for trade pubs. These channels are for other named leaders.

Trade pubs split into three lanes by audience and subject:

#### Lane A — Engineering trade pubs

**Outlets:** The New Stack, DevOps Digest, ComputerWeekly (engineering coverage)

**Audience:** Practitioners and engineering leaders. Technically literate. Will scrutinize claims that don't hold up architecturally.

**Eligible authors:**
- Justice Erolin (CTO)
- David Von Dollen (Head of AI)
- Nacho Morales (VP of Technology)
- Rocío Belfiore (Chief Innovation Officer)

**Angle logic:** Architectural shifts, implementation patterns, tooling changes, AI in the engineering workflow. Evidence skews toward technical patterns observed across client work or Dev Barometer engineering data.

**Tone delta:** Practitioner register. No marketing language. Technical specificity is the credibility currency.

#### Lane B — Business and strategy trade pubs

**Outlets:** ComputerWeekly (strategy coverage) and similar business-focused trade publications.

**Audience:** Senior tech-business decision-makers — CIOs, CTOs in their buyer capacity, heads of digital transformation.

**Eligible authors:**
- Darren Shimkus (President, North America)
- Pablo Chamorro (Chief Product Officer)
- Guillermo Carreras (Head of Agile & Digital Transformation)

**Angle logic:** Nearshoring economics, software development strategy, build-vs-buy, vendor models, AI adoption at the organizational level.

**Tone delta:** Business register. ROI-aware. Less hands-on-technical.

#### Lane C — Talent and HR trade pubs

**Outlets:** HR Brew

**Audience:** CHROs, heads of talent, people ops leaders.

**Eligible authors:**
- Luis Paiva (Chief People & Operations Officer)
- Natalia Rodriguez (VP of Talent Acquisition)

**Angle logic:** Workforce shifts, talent market dynamics, remote and distributed work, reskilling, hiring under AI pressure.

**Tone delta:** People-leader register. Evidence drawn from talent data and workforce trends rather than engineering metrics.

**Cross-lane authors:**
- Guillermo Carreras can also work in Lane A (engineering trade pubs)

#### Shared trade pub baseline

**Length:** ~1,000 words.

**Approval chain:** Media Manager + PR Specialist.

**Ready-to-publish definition:** Meets baseline PR op-ed criteria, byline matches lane, technical or business specificity matches outlet's audience, Media Manager and PR Specialist approval secured.
