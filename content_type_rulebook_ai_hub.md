# Content Type Rulebook — AI Hub Articles

**Document role:** This is one entry in the Content Type Rulebook (Document 1 of 6). It defines what a publishable AI Hub article looks like for BairesDev's owned blog. Per-author voice is not centralized for this format — Estef writes end-to-end in a unified expert voice (confidence, experience, authority) and the byline is assigned downstream from a roster. Topic relevance ties to the 2026 Strategic Topic Priorities (Document 3) and proprietary insight events surfaced via the Source Asset Index (Document 5). Author-to-topic matching uses a subset of the broader author roster (Document 4).

---

## Wizard Scope (applies to this entry and all rulebook entries)

The wizard's role is upstream of drafting and editing. It surfaces angles, vets pitches, checks quality against the criteria below, suggests authors, identifies evidence and source assets, and flags content opportunities.

The wizard does not:
- Draft article copy
- Edit drafts line-by-line
- Apply voice profiles to generate prose
- Replicate the work of dedicated skills (fellows-article-editing, graphic-assets-suggestions, webinar-blog)

When a quality criterion below would be executed via a skill or by Estef directly, the wizard's job is to verify the criterion has been met after the work runs, not to execute it.

For AI Hub articles specifically: Estef writes the article end-to-end. The wizard's role is to surface and refine the topic, identify proprietary triggers and supporting evidence, suggest plausible authors for byline assignment, and verify the resulting draft hits the publication criteria below. The wizard does not draft the piece.

---

## 1. Format Definition

An AI Hub article is a long-form, expert-voiced piece published on BairesDev's owned blog (the AI Hub section). It is the company's primary owned-channel vehicle for demonstrating authority on AI in the context of software engineering and technology leadership.

**Strategic job of this format:**

To position BairesDev as a trusted vendor in the AI development space — first by establishing brand authority through differentiated thinking, second by supporting conversion. AI Hub articles do not chase SEO keywords. They are not born out of content gaps surfaced by keyword research. **Content is born out of solid, timely opportunities to talk about AI impact**, anchored in proprietary insight or expert observation.

**Topic territory:**

Strictly AI-related themes — AI development, AI consultancy, AI strategy, AI implementation, AI readiness, AI governance, AI in engineering practice, AI's impact on talent and roles, scaling AI in the enterprise, securing AI systems. Proprietary findings on non-AI topics (nearshoring economics, engineering team composition outside the AI lens, etc.) belong in PR op-eds, not here.

**An AI Hub article is:**

- A long-form expert piece (2,000–2,800 words)
- Authored end-to-end by Estef, with a senior-leader byline assigned after drafting
- Anchored in proprietary insight (Dev Barometer, internal surveys, internal research, client work patterns) or in a current shift the company can credibly comment on
- Designed to give a senior decision-maker something differentiated to "pick up"
- AEO-optimized but not SEO-optimized

**An AI Hub article is not:**

- A keyword-driven SEO piece
- A generic explainer ("What is AI?", "How does machine learning work?")
- A foundational "welcome to AI" piece (that was the 2025 mode; 2026 has moved past it)
- A piece on a non-AI topic, even if BairesDev has proprietary data on it
- Authored in any individual leader's voice (the byline is assigned, not voice-shaping)

## 2. The Wizard's Role for AI Hub Articles

Three jobs, in order of priority:

1. **Surface the topic.** Cross-reference proprietary insight events (Dev Barometer waves, internal surveys, research) against the live AI conversation senior decision-makers are engaging with. The wizard verifies this through web search and review of:
 - **Current reporting** in business and tech press (HBR, MIT Sloan Management Review, The Information, Wired, Bloomberg, Fortune, The New York Times tech coverage, The Wall Street Journal, etc.)
 - **Executive commentary and statements** from senior leaders publishing on AI topics (LinkedIn articles, op-eds, keynote excerpts, podcast appearances)
 - **Industry research** from analysts and consultancies (Gartner, McKinsey, BCG, Deloitte, Forrester, MIT Sloan, Stanford HAI, etc.)
 - **Tech company publications** from non-competitors with strong thought leadership presence (IBM, Anthropic, Substack-published technologists, etc.)
 - **Published fellows' content** from BairesDev's own corpus, indexed via the Source Asset Index (Document 5)
 
 **Deprioritize:** content from direct or adjacent competitors — other nearshore / offshore / staff augmentation firms publishing similar AI thought leadership. Their coverage shouldn't shape what BairesDev says next.
 
 The goal of the scan is to confirm the trigger lands somewhere the audience is already paying attention, and to identify what BairesDev's finding adds that the existing conversation doesn't have. The wizard should never chase the discourse — it should locate where the finding contributes something the discourse is missing.
2. **Refine the angle, title, and structure.** Sharpen the topic into a publishable angle. Stress-test the proposed title against the 2026 title pattern (§6). Propose section logic and identify where evidence, expert input, or external corroboration is needed. The wizard does not draft the article — Estef does. The wizard's contribution is at the editorial-strategy level: what the piece should argue, where the evidence comes from, how it's structured, what the title should do.
3. **Suggest plausible authors for byline assignment.** Offer the SEO team's roster a shortlist of senior leaders whose expertise credibly matches the topic. Estef makes the final call on assignment.
4. **Verify the draft hits publication criteria.** Once Estef has drafted, the wizard checks the piece against the criteria in §11 — flagging gaps in evidence, voice register, AEO, audience consistency, or title structure for Estef to address.

**Priority clarification:** The top priority is not topic-to-author matching. The top priority is producing a quality, differentiated, current article that senior decision-makers will pick up. The author byline is a credibility stamp added once the topic is solid. A great topic without a clear author byline is still worth pursuing; a thin topic with a perfect author match is not.

## 3. The 2025 → 2026 Shift in Editorial Posture

This is critical context for the wizard.

**2025 posture:** Welcome business leaders into the AI universe. Explore foundational concepts. Title patterns leaned toward "What is X?", "A no-nonsense guide to Y for business leaders." Foundational fluency was the goal.

**2026 posture:** Business leaders have already entered the AI universe. Organizational AI adoption has progressed enough that the same audience now faces specific operational challenges — scaling, governance, validation, evolving roles, accountability gaps, model performance, oversight failure modes. The audience is still business leaders entering the AI universe, but at a **more advanced level.** Foundational explainers are no longer differentiated; they are catch-up content.

The wizard should reject angles that read as 2025-style foundational explainers and push toward 2026-style operational specificity.

## 4. What Makes a Strong Topic — The Trigger Test

A topic is strong when an internal source unlocks a conversation that an experienced POV can credibly extend, and that the audience is asking about right now.

The three components, all required:

1. **A proprietary or vantage-point trigger.** Dev Barometer findings, internal survey results, research, or a pattern the company has observed across client work. The data may be substantial (1,000+ Dev Barometer respondents) or modest (an 11-person internal survey). Sample size is not the disqualifier. The trigger's job is to unlock the conversation; the article's value comes from what the expert lens adds on top.
2. **A live question senior decision-makers are asking.** The topic must connect to something the audience is actively wrestling with — scaling AI, governance, validation, new role definitions, oversight, accuracy, accountability, and similar 2026 operational challenges. The wizard verifies this is a live question by scanning current reporting in business and tech press (HBR, MIT Sloan, The Information, Wired, Bloomberg, Fortune, etc.), executive commentary on AI, industry research from analysts (Gartner, McKinsey, BCG, MIT Sloan, Stanford HAI, etc.), and publications from credible non-competitor tech companies (IBM, Anthropic, Substack-published technologists, etc.). Direct or adjacent competitors are deprioritized in this scan. If the connection to a live question isn't visible after the scan, the topic isn't ready.
3. **A credible expert lens to extend it.** Someone in the company — across the broader leader roster — must be able to interpret the finding with judgment, experience, and pattern recognition. Without that lens, the article reduces to data presentation, which doesn't earn the format.

### Strong example

A Dev Barometer finding (1,000+ respondents) that junior developers are skipping fundamentals because they rely entirely on AI to "vibe" code. An AI or talent expert is summoned to interpret this — what it means for team leaders, what it implies for entry-level hiring, what risks loom over junior talent's career trajectories. The data unlocks the conversation; the expert extends it; the audience cares because junior talent and entry jobs are a current source of organizational anxiety.

### Weaker example that still passes

An internal survey of 11 software developers surfaces a finding that AI implementation governance is a recurring friction point. The sample is small but the insight is actionable. The article opens with the survey as the conversation-starter ("35% of surveyed software developers in a recent company survey said…"), then deepens the analysis through investigation, expert input, and external corroboration. It works because the finding hits a thread that's already alive in the discourse and an expert can credibly extend it.

### Rejection patterns

The wizard should flag a topic when:

- The finding doesn't connect to a live question senior decision-makers are asking
- No leader in the roster can credibly extend the finding with judgment or pattern recognition
- The finding only confirms widely-known consensus without a new angle on why or what it means
- The angle reads as a 2025-style foundational explainer
- The framing forces pure abstraction or speculation, with no room for grounded interpretation
- The topic isn't AI-related (route to PR op-eds instead)

## 5. Audience

Senior tech decision-makers — the same ICP as PR op-eds — but with a softer threshold on technical specificity and a wider band of role types.

**Audience composition:**

- CTOs, VPs of Engineering, Heads of Platform, Chief Data/AI Officers
- CIOs, Directors of Digital Transformation
- Senior business leaders making AI investment, vendor, or adoption decisions
- Decision-makers entering the AI universe at a more advanced operational level

**Audience disqualifiers (same logic as fellows' articles, applied here):**

The audience is technologists in the shape of senior tech decision-makers. Disqualifying mismatches are role types where AI's operational challenges don't translate — procurement specialists, HR analysts (in their HR capacity), legal advisors, marketing generalists, and similar. The wizard should reject angles oriented toward these readers.

**Difference from fellows' articles:** fellows' articles can speak to senior software engineers as readily as to CTOs. AI Hub leans more toward business-leader framing — senior technical leaders are still in the audience, but the language register translates AI concepts for business action rather than for engineering-practice scrutiny.

## 6. Title Pattern (2026)

A 2026 AI Hub title must do three things:

1. **Pinpoint the challenge** — name it specifically, not generically
2. **Express relevance or urgency** — why this matters now
3. **Point to either the solution or the scope of the challenge** — what the reader will get from the piece

### Worked examples

- *"AI Oversight: 4 Failure Modes That Are Quietly Undermining Your AI Systems"*
 - Challenge: oversight failure modes
 - Urgency: "quietly undermining"
 - Scope: 4 failure modes
- *"AI Model Performance: What Metrics Matter for Business Leaders"*
 - Challenge: which metrics matter
 - Urgency: implicit (decision-makers need this framework now)
 - Solution: the metrics framework

### Title check (apply to every draft title)

- Is the challenge specific and named?
- Is there a "why now" — urgency, stakes, or live shift?
- Does the reader know what they'll get (solution, framework, scope, criteria)?
- Would this title have worked unchanged in 2025? If yes, it's foundational; rework toward operational specificity.

## 7. Structure and Length

**Length:** 2,000–2,800 words.

The long format allows deeper dives into technical or complex AI explanations and leaves room to layer proprietary insight, expert input, external corroboration, and actionable framing.

**Section logic:**

The general PR op-ed structure (compact intro, H2 sections, optional framework section, lean conclusion) applies as a starting point, but section count flexes upward. Long-format AI Hub articles often need:

- Compact intro establishing the trigger and the stakes
- Multiple H2 sections breaking down the challenge, the evidence, the implications, and the recommended approach
- H3 subsections where technical depth or framework granularity warrants it
- A concluding section that doesn't just summarize — it lands a stance and gives the reader something to act on

**Headings:** Title case for H2s and H3s.

## 8. Voice

There is no per-author voice profile applied to AI Hub articles. Estef writes end-to-end in a unified expert register; the byline is assigned afterward.

**The expert voice register (apply to all AI Hub drafts):**

- **Confidence** — willing to take a stance, name the challenge, and prescribe direction
- **Experience** — claims and framings are anchored in observed patterns, client work, or proprietary findings, not in abstraction
- **Authority** — the article speaks as a company that has seen this challenge play out across many environments and has earned the right to interpret it

The expert voice does not flatten into corporate neutrality. It carries opinion, point of view, and a clear sense that the writer has been close to the problem.

**Universal hard editorial rules apply** (same as PR op-eds and fellows' articles):

- No contrastive substitution
- No em dashes unless structurally necessary
- No templated AI phrases
- No redundancy across sections
- Title case for H2s and H3s
- No colons where avoidable

**AEO optimization:**

- Question-format headings with direct answer paragraphs where appropriate
- Executive summary and TL;DR for LLM retrieval
- Stat sourcing: external claims linked to verifiable sources; proprietary findings attributed to the internal source (Dev Barometer Q[X] 20[XX], internal survey, etc.)

**SEO:**

- Not optimized for keywords. The format does not chase search demand.

## 10. Approval and Publication

**Draft approval:** The assigned author reviews the draft, provides comments or approval. Their feedback may shape final edits.

**Editorial control:** Estef owns the editorial decisions through draft completion. The author reviews for accuracy and credibility from their vantage point but does not rewrite.

**Publication:** Once the author approves, the article is scheduled.

## 11. Ready-to-Publish Verification (Wizard Checklist)

Before publication, the wizard verifies the following criteria are met. Estef executes the drafting and editorial work; the wizard confirms each criterion has been satisfied:

1. The topic passed the trigger test (proprietary or vantage-point trigger + live question + credible expert lens)
2. The topic is strictly AI-related
3. The angle is operationally specific (2026 posture), not foundational (2025 posture)
4. The title meets the three-component pattern: pinpoint the challenge, express relevance or urgency, point to solution or scope
5. Length lands in 2,000–2,800 words; structure flexes section count to match content depth
6. Universal hard editorial rules are applied
7. The expert voice register (confidence, experience, authority) is consistent throughout
8. AEO pass is complete (executive summary, TL;DR, question-format headings where appropriate, redundancy eliminated)
9. Every external stat has a verified source linked; proprietary findings are attributed to the internal source
10. Audience is consistently senior tech decision-makers; no drift into mismatched role types
11. The byline is assigned and the author has approved the draft
12. Graphic asset suggestions are produced via the dedicated skill where applicable

If any criterion fails verification, the wizard surfaces the gap so Estef or the relevant skill can address it before publication.

## 12. Open Items and Flags

Items surfaced during this draft that need resolution as the other documents come together:

- **Source asset indexing for AI Hub.** The Source Asset Index (Document 5) needs to capture proprietary insight events as triggers for this format — Dev Barometer waves with key findings, internal surveys (with sample sizes and key insights), client-work pattern observations. The wizard's ability to surface AI Hub topics depends entirely on how well this index is structured. Each entry should include: source name, date, sample size or scope, key findings, and AI-relevance flag.
- **Author roster expansion.** The 25 leaders listed are partial. The full roster lives with the SEO team and can be requested when Document 4 (Author Roster) is built. For Document 4, the AI Hub author pool should be flagged as the broadest pool across all formats — most senior leaders qualify, with assignment driven by topic-to-expertise fit rather than channel exclusivity.
- **2026 posture as a strategic anchor.** The 2025 → 2026 shift (foundational → operational) is essentially a strategic editorial principle. Worth confirming whether this lives only in the AI Hub rulebook or surfaces in Document 3 (2026 Strategic Topic Priorities) as a cross-format orientation point.
- **AI Hub vs. PR op-ed routing.** Some proprietary findings could plausibly become either an AI Hub article (if AI-related) or a PR op-ed (if framed for external authority and timeliness). The Intersection Matrix (Document 2) should capture the routing logic — AI topic + owned channel + business-leader audience → AI Hub; AI topic + external channel + leader byline → PR op-ed.
- **AI Hub vs. fellows' articles routing.** Same proprietary finding could yield an AI Hub article (with assigned byline, business-leader register) or a fellows' article (with fellow byline, technologist register). Routing logic depends on (a) audience register and (b) whether the byline is internal-assigned or fellow-pitched. Worth capturing in the Intersection Matrix.
