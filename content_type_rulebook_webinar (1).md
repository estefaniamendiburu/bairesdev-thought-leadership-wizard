# Content Type Rulebook — Webinar Blog Posts

**Document role:** This is one entry in the Content Type Rulebook (Document 1 of 6). It defines what a publishable webinar blog post looks like for BairesDev's owned blog. Webinar blog posts are recap-style content derived from BairesDev-hosted webinars and broadcast across LinkedIn Events, YouTube, and the BairesDev Blog. Bob Leibholz is the default byline regardless of who hosts the underlying webinar. Topic origination is controlled by Bob's team; angle extraction is shared with the wizard.

---

## Wizard Scope (applies to this entry and all rulebook entries)

The wizard's role is upstream of drafting and editing. It surfaces angles, vets pitches, checks quality against the criteria below, suggests authors, identifies evidence and source assets, and flags content opportunities.

The wizard does not:
- Draft article copy
- Edit drafts line-by-line
- Apply voice profiles to generate prose
- Replicate the work of dedicated skills (fellows-article-editing, graphic-assets-suggestions, webinar-blog)

When a quality criterion below would be executed via a skill or by Estef directly, the wizard's job is to verify the criterion has been met after the work runs, not to execute it.

For webinar blog posts specifically: the dedicated `webinar-blog` skill handles the recap drafting (insight extraction, structure, section drafting, quote usage). The dedicated `graphic-assets-suggestions` skill handles visual asset cards. The wizard's role is to surface the truer angle from the transcript when it diverges from the webinar's stated billing, identify the editorial decisions that need to be made before drafting begins, and verify the resulting recap hits the publication criteria below — not to execute the recap drafting itself.

---

## 1. Format Definition

A webinar blog post is a long-form recap of a BairesDev-hosted webinar, published on the BairesDev Blog under Bob Leibholz's byline. It captures who participated, what they said, the main takeaways, and lands a soft CTA at the end.

**Strategic job of this format:**

The webinar itself runs across three audiences in three different environments:
- **LinkedIn Events** — live broadcast (business networking audience)
- **YouTube** — edited recording, promoted via social media (broader media/social audience)
- **BairesDev Blog** — written recap (website visitor audience)

The blog post serves the third leg. It captures the substance of the webinar in a form that website visitors — BairesDev's ICP of senior tech decision-makers — can read, reference, and share. It also extends the lifespan of webinar content beyond its live moment.

**A webinar blog post is:**

- A long-form recap of a BairesDev-hosted webinar (1,500–1,800 words)
- Authored by Estef (with the `webinar-blog` skill), published under Bob Leibholz's byline
- Anchored in the actual conversation that happened, not the webinar's promotional billing
- Structured around what the panelists said, who said it, and what the audience should take away
- Closed with a soft CTA inviting contact with BairesDev

**A webinar blog post is not:**

- A transcript or near-transcript reproduction
- An opinion piece authored by Bob (Bob is the producer-anchor, not the opinion holder)
- A piece that follows the webinar's stated title verbatim if the actual conversation diverged
- A piece authored by anyone other than Bob, regardless of who hosted the underlying webinar

---

## 2. The Wizard's Role for Webinar Blog Posts

Three jobs:

1. **Surface the truer angle from the transcript.** The webinar's stated title and the article's true angle often diverge. Bob's team picks the webinar's promotional billing; the conversation that actually happens is shaped by the panelists, the moderator, and the live dynamic. The wizard reads the transcript, identifies the through-line, and proposes an article angle that reflects what the conversation actually was — not what it was advertised as. Estef makes the editorial call; Bob's team approves.

 **Canonical example of title divergence:** A webinar billed as *"Cloud & Development Trends 2026: From Cloud Native to AI-First Engineering"* produced an article titled *"From AI Pilot to Production: The Organizational Shifts Engineering Teams Can't Skip — Webinar Replay."* Same content; sharper, truer angle. The wizard should expect this kind of gap and surface it.

2. **Identify editorial decisions that need to be made before drafting.** Things like: which panelist quotes are powerful enough to keep verbatim vs. synthesize? Which claims warrant graphic assets (checklists, diagrams, step-by-step processes, Venn diagrams)? Where are the natural section breaks in the conversation? The wizard surfaces these as decisions for Estef to make; the `webinar-blog` skill executes against the decisions.

3. **Verify the recap hits publication criteria.** Once the `webinar-blog` skill has drafted, the wizard checks the recap against the criteria in §10 — flagging gaps in attribution accuracy, audience consistency, structure, voice register (Bob as reporter-anchor), or CTA tailoring for Estef and the skill to address.

The wizard does not draft the recap. It does not extract and structure quotes. It does not produce graphic asset cards. Those are the skill's job.

## 3. The Reporter-Anchor Voice (Bob's Byline)

Bob Leibholz is always the byline regardless of who hosted the underlying webinar. Bob's role on the blog post is **producer-anchor**, not opinion-holder.

**Voice register:**

- **Reporter framing.** *"In our most recent webinar, hosted by [name], panelists [name], [name], and [name] shed light on the most important shifts in [topic]…"*
- **Third-person attribution throughout.** The panelists hold the opinions; Bob's voice introduces, frames, and synthesizes what they said.
- **No first-person opinion from Bob.** Bob is not authoring a position piece. He is reporting on what transpired.
- **Authority through curation.** Bob's voice carries weight because he is selecting what mattered from the conversation, not because he is taking a stance.

This voice register is consistent regardless of webinar topic. Variation comes from the topic and panelists, not from Bob's voice.

---

## 4. Audience

**ICP-aligned:** senior tech decision-makers — CTOs, VPs of Engineering, Heads of Platform, CIOs, senior business leaders making technology adoption decisions. Same audience as PR op-eds and AI Hub.

**Why ICP-alignment holds even with three distribution channels:** The webinar is broadcast on LinkedIn Events, YouTube, and the BairesDev Blog — three different audiences in different environments. The blog version specifically reaches BairesDev's website visitors, who are concentrated in the ICP. Additionally, the panelists themselves typically operate at a register that suits senior decision-makers — they share industry and company wisdom, high-level observations, and leadership-level guidance. They are not walking through implementation tutorials or explaining processes step-by-step. The article inherits that register.

**Audience disqualifiers** (same logic as fellows' and AI Hub articles): non-technical role types where the panelists' framings don't translate — procurement specialists, HR analysts in HR capacity, legal advisors, marketing generalists.

---

## 5. Topic Origination — Reactive

Bob's team picks the webinar topics on their own logic. Estef does not shape topic selection; the wizard does not propose webinar topics. The webinar happens, and the article responds to it.

This makes webinar blog posts a **reactive format on origination, proactive on angle extraction.** The topic is given; the angle is the editorial work.

The wizard does not evaluate whether a webinar topic was a good idea for the format. It accepts the webinar as-is and focuses on extracting the truer angle from what happened.

---

## 6. Angle Extraction — Where the Wizard Adds the Most Value

The webinar's promotional title is a marketing artifact. The actual conversation is the editorial substance. These diverge frequently enough that angle extraction is the wizard's highest-leverage contribution to this format.

**The wizard's process:**

1. Read the transcript end-to-end
2. Identify the dominant through-line of what was actually discussed (not what was advertised)
3. Identify secondary threads worth surfacing in sections but not as the headline
4. Propose an article title that reflects the true angle, using the same title quality criteria as AI Hub where applicable (pinpoint the challenge, express relevance or urgency, point to solution or scope)
5. Surface the proposed angle and title to Estef with reasoning — what the webinar billed itself as, what the actual conversation was, why the proposed angle is sharper

**Editorial authority:** Estef makes the call on whether to follow the truer angle or stay closer to the original billing. Bob's team approves the final result. If the angle diverges sharply from the billing, Estef may need to explain the editorial choice during approval — that's expected. The wizard's job is to surface the option, not to enforce it.

**When angles align:** Sometimes the webinar's billing and the actual conversation match closely. The wizard should still confirm this rather than assume — read the transcript, verify the through-line, and report alignment.

## 7. Structure and Length

**Length:** 1,500–1,800 words.

**Section logic:**

- **Title** (reflects the true angle, may diverge from webinar billing)
- **Dek** — short standfirst summarizing what the article covers
- **Intro** — introduces the panelists by name and role, explains what they discussed and why it matters for business and tech leaders
- **3–5 main sections** breaking down the webinar's substance, each typically organized around a key takeaway, panelist insight, or thematic thread
- **Quick conclusion** with a soft CTA inviting contact with BairesDev

**Headings:** Title case for H2s and H3s.

---

## 8. Quote Usage and Attribution

**Source of truth:** The webinar transcript. Every claim attributed to a panelist must be traceable to what they actually said.

**Quote usage decisions** (the `webinar-blog` skill executes these; Estef and the wizard inform them):

- **Verbatim quotes** for lines that are powerful, claim-anchoring, or distinctively phrased — kept as-is
- **Synthesized quotes** for lines that are too long for direct quotation but capture an idea worth attributing — paraphrased while preserving the panelist's intent and clearly attributed
- **Powerful or relevant claims** justify verbatim treatment even when synthesis would be tighter — the editorial call is whether the line's force depends on its exact phrasing

**Attribution discipline:** Panelists are named with their role and company at first mention. Subsequent references can use first name or last name per editorial convention. No claim should appear unattributed if it originated with a specific panelist.

**Panelist review:** Panelists do not review their attributed quotes before publication. Bob's team is the only approval gate. This means the burden of accurate attribution sits entirely with Estef and the `webinar-blog` skill — get it right the first time.

---

## 9. CTA Pattern

The article closes with a soft CTA inviting contact with BairesDev. The CTA is a **template tailored to the topic** rather than a fully bespoke close.

**Tailoring logic:** The CTA references the topic the webinar covered (e.g., AI implementation, cloud architecture, engineering team transformation) and points toward BairesDev's relevant capability area. The tone is invitational, not aggressive — *"To explore how [topic capability] can support your team, get in touch with our team"*-style framing rather than hard sales language.

The CTA should not be the loudest thing in the conclusion. The takeaway from the webinar carries first; the CTA closes.

## 10. Ready-to-Publish Verification (Wizard Checklist)

Before publication, the wizard verifies the following criteria are met. Estef and the `webinar-blog` skill execute the drafting; the wizard confirms each criterion has been satisfied:

1. The article angle reflects the truer through-line from the transcript, not just the webinar's promotional billing (or alignment between billing and conversation has been verified)
2. Title meets quality criteria — pinpoints the topic, signals relevance, indicates what the reader will get
3. All panelists are named with role and company at first mention
4. Every attributed claim is traceable to the transcript
5. Verbatim quotes are kept where the line's force depends on exact phrasing; long lines are synthesized
6. Bob's voice holds the reporter-anchor register throughout — no first-person opinion from him
7. The audience is consistently senior tech decision-makers; no drift into mismatched role types
8. Universal hard editorial rules are applied (no contrastive substitution, no em dashes unless structurally necessary, no templated AI phrases, title case for H2s and H3s, no colons where avoidable, no redundancy across sections)
9. Length lands in 1,500–1,800 words
10. 3–5 main sections; intro introduces panelists; conclusion lands the soft CTA tailored to topic
11. Graphic asset suggestions are produced via the `graphic-assets-suggestions` skill where panelist points warrant visual treatment (checklists, diagrams, step-by-step processes, Venn diagrams)
12. Bob's team has approved the draft

If any criterion fails verification, the wizard surfaces the gap so Estef or the relevant skill can address it before publication.

## 11. Open Items and Flags

Items surfaced during this draft that need resolution as the other documents come together:

- **Webinar transcripts as source assets.** Webinar transcripts (and the resulting blog posts) should be indexed in the Source Asset Index (Document 5) — not just as past output, but as a feed of panelist insights and named framings the wizard can mine when proactively flagging angles for PR op-eds and AI Hub articles. Especially when external panelists from non-competitor companies share noteworthy framings, those become reference points the wizard can cite later.
- **Bob's authorship anchor.** Bob is the only author for this format across all webinars. Worth capturing in Document 4 (Author Roster) that Bob's profile is unique — he's an author who carries one specific format and one specific voice register (reporter-anchor), distinct from how other leaders show up across multiple formats.
- **CTA template library.** The CTA is tailored per topic but follows a template. Worth capturing the template (or templates) somewhere — possibly in Document 6 (Services and Solutions Reference) since the CTA links to capability areas. This would let the wizard suggest CTA framings aligned to the topic without having to invent them.
- **Cross-format intelligence loop.** Webinar transcripts feed angle ideas to PR op-eds and AI Hub. Published fellows' articles also feed angle ideas to PR op-eds and AI Hub. This is a recurring pattern across formats — owned content becoming source material for other owned content. Worth capturing as a structural principle when the documents are unified, possibly in Document 5's design.
- **Title quality criteria reuse.** The 2026 title pattern from the AI Hub entry (pinpoint the challenge, express relevance or urgency, point to solution or scope) applies cleanly here too. Worth confirming during the cross-format sweep whether title quality criteria should be lifted to a shared editorial principle rather than restated per format.
