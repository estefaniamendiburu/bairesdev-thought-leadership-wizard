# Content Type Rulebook — Fellows' Articles

**Document role:** This is one entry in the Content Type Rulebook (Document 1 of 6). It defines what a publishable fellows' article looks like and how the wizard should reason about fellow-pitched content. Voice profiles for fellows are not centralized — fellows write in their own voice, and the editorial mandate is to preserve it. Topic relevance to BairesDev's strategic priorities (Document 3) applies as a directional filter, not a control point — fellows self-select topics. Published fellows' articles are a feed into the Source Asset Index (Document 5) for the other formats.

---

## Wizard Scope (applies to this entry and all rulebook entries)

The wizard's role is upstream of drafting and editing. It surfaces angles, vets pitches, checks quality against the criteria below, suggests authors, identifies evidence and source assets, and flags content opportunities.

The wizard does not:
- Draft article copy
- Edit drafts line-by-line
- Apply voice profiles to generate prose
- Replicate the work of dedicated skills (fellows-article-editing, graphic-assets-suggestions, webinar-blog)

When a quality criterion below would be executed via a skill or by Estef directly, the wizard's job is to verify the criterion has been met after the work runs, not to execute it.

For fellows' articles specifically: the dedicated `fellows-article-editing` skill handles editing execution (logical structure check, audience check, hard editorial rules, humanizing pass, AEO pass, redundancy pass, stat sourcing). The dedicated `graphic-assets-suggestions` skill handles visual asset cards. The wizard's role is to vet the incoming pitch against the criteria below, surface relevant context to inform the editing skill, and verify publication readiness once the skill has run — not to execute the editing passes itself.

## 1. Format Definition

A fellows' article is a long-form piece authored by a paid external expert — typically a CTO, Chief Data/AI Officer, or equivalent senior technology leader at a recognized company — published on the BairesDev Blog under their byline.

**Examples of fellows BairesDev has published:**
- Jeremy Freeman, CTO at Caring.com
- Kapil Bakshi, CTO at Cisco
- Ayman Shoukry, CTO at Specright
- Charley Boyle, Chief Data & Analytics Officer at Wipfli
- Jim Moore, CTO at HR Acuity
- Rob Teegarden, CTO at Clearer.io
- Arun Nandi, Chief Data and AI Officer at Carrier

**Strategic job of this format:**

Fellows' articles exist to bring authority and credibility to BairesDev's blog and digital presence by injecting expert voices and lived knowledge that BairesDev's in-house content cannot replicate. The format was introduced to address a specific problem: in-house blog content was technically competent but generic — it wasn't saying anything different or outstanding. Fellows fix that by contributing perspective only senior practitioners with decades of operating experience can offer.

Secondary benefit: fellows promote their own published articles on social channels, which expands distribution beyond BairesDev's owned audience.

**A fellows' article is:**
- A long-form expert piece (1,500–2,800 words)
- Authored by a senior technology leader, in their voice, under their byline
- Anchored in technological challenges, engineering team dynamics, AI's impact on organizations, or frameworks for solving complex tech leadership problems
- Grounded in the fellow's personal experience — failures, lessons, judgment calls, observed patterns
- Read by senior technology decision-makers and the practitioners who work for them

**A fellows' article is not:**
- A topic the editorial team assigns (fellows pitch us, not the other way around)
- A piece BairesDev shapes around its services or commercial priorities
- Generic best-practices content with no first-person grounding
- A political, religious, or confrontational stance against another organization or government

## 2. The Wizard's Role for Fellows' Articles Is Reactive

This format is structurally different from PR op-eds, AI Hub articles, and webinar blog posts. For those three formats, BairesDev controls topic selection, author assignment, and timing. **For fellows' articles, BairesDev does not control any of those.** Fellows self-select topics and send pitches when they choose.

The wizard's role for this format is therefore **reactive across three jobs**:

1. **Vetting incoming pitches** — Is the topic fit-for-publication? If not, what feedback returns it to fit?
2. **Editing submitted drafts** — Light by default, structural when needed. Producing the publication complements (dek, graphic asset suggestions, AEO pass).
3. **Mining published fellows' content as a source asset** — Surfaced patterns, framings, and insights from published fellows' articles feed back into the *proactive* side of the wizard for PR op-eds and AI Hub articles. A fellow's piece on AI accountability can surface a thread Nacho should comment on. A fellow's piece on platform engineering can seed a Forbes angle. This indexing belongs in the Source Asset Index (Document 5).

The wizard does not propose fellows' article topics. It does not match topics to fellows. The pitch is the input; the wizard responds to it.

## 3. Vetting a Pitch — What Makes a Topic Fit

When a fellow sends a pitch (typically a tentative title and short description), the wizard evaluates fit on two primary dimensions and a few secondary ones.

### 3.1 Audience type fit (primary)

The audience is **technologists**, not business generalists. The seniority level can vary — a senior software engineer may engage with a CTO's article just as readily as another CTO would, because the pains and context overlap. The disqualifying mismatch is **role type**, not seniority.

- **Strong fit:** angles oriented toward CTOs, VPs of Engineering, Heads of Platform, Chief Data/AI Officers, engineering directors, senior engineers
- **Weak or unfit:** angles oriented toward marketers, sales leaders, business generalists, or non-technical buyers

A pitch oriented toward the wrong role type is the clearest red flag.

### 3.2 Clarity of the challenge or problem (primary)

The tentative title should make the challenge or problem immediately visible. The reader should be able to tell what they'll learn and why it matters from the title alone.

- **Strong example:** *"What Makes an Engineering Team High-Performing in the AI Era: The Updated Playbook"* — reveals that AI has shifted the definition of high-performing teams, and signals structured advice from the author.
- **Weak example:** *"ROI from AI"* — directionally legible, but the reader can't tell what they'll learn, what stance the author will take, or why this piece exists. Vague or generic pitches get sent back for rethinking.

### 3.3 Secondary fit signals

- **Topic territory:** engineering team dynamics and composition, AI's impact on technology organizations, scaling AI in the enterprise, securing enterprise AI, frameworks for technology leadership decisions, the changing shape of engineering work. These are the lanes fellows write in well.
- **Room for personal experience:** Does the topic let the fellow draw on lived operating experience? If the topic forces them into pure abstraction or speculation, it's a weak fit even if technically interesting.
- **Opinion latitude:** Opinionated stands are welcome and valued — thoughts on the future of technology, engineering teams, AI's challenges, emerging shifts. The wizard should not flatten conviction.
- **Brand-safety check:** No political, religious, or confrontational angles against organizations or governments. These are non-negotiable rejections.

## 4. Vetting a Pitch — How the Wizard Responds

Two response patterns:

**Pattern A — Topic is fit:** "Good topic, here are some recommendations." Recommendations always include the standing one: integrate personal experience. Specifically — challenges, failures, lessons, insights, growth moments. This is the single highest-leverage editorial intervention for this format and applies to nearly every pitch.

**Pattern B — Topic is unfit:** "Not a fitting topic for our blog or for our audience, can you orient this towards X, Y, or Z?" The redirect names alternative angles the fellow could pivot to, ideally drawing on the same expertise but reframing for audience fit and challenge clarity.

The redirect is constructive. The goal is to land a publication, not gatekeep.

## 5. Editing Criteria — Default Light Touch

The editing of fellows' drafts is executed by the dedicated `fellows-article-editing` skill (and Estef's editorial judgment, supported by the skill). The wizard's role here is to **inform the editing skill with relevant context** (e.g., flagging that the pitch came in oriented toward the wrong audience type, or that the topic overlaps with a recently published fellow's piece) and to **verify the criteria below have been met** before publication.

The editing philosophy that the skill executes against: **preserve the author's voice and language as much as possible.** Personality traits in the writing are an asset, not a bug.

### 5.1 Quality criteria the editing skill must hit (default light pass)

- **Logical structure** — The piece holds to a single central story. Each section supports it.
- **Audience consistency** — The piece speaks to senior technology decision-makers throughout. No drift into adjacent audiences (marketers, procurement specialists, HR analysts in HR capacity, legal advisors).
- **Idea clarity** — The author's ideas are legible. Transitions and structural cues surface them where needed.
- **Universal hard editorial rules applied** — No contrastive substitution ("it's not X, it's Y"), no em dashes unless structurally necessary, title case for H2s and H3s, no colons where avoidable, no templated AI phrases, no redundancy across sections.
- **Humanizing pass complete** — AI-sounding tics removed. Beyond the universal rules, recurring tells in fellows' drafts include *"after the fact,"* *"the organizations who win are the ones…"*, and similar templated structures.
- **AEO pass complete** — Question-format headings with direct answer paragraphs where appropriate, executive summary, TL;DR placement.
- **Redundancy pass complete** — Restated points across sections eliminated.
- **Stat sourcing verified** — Every external data claim has a verifiable source linked. Experience-grounded claims do not need external sources — *"in my 10+ years as [role], I've seen more than half of engineering teams…"* passes as-is. The fellow's experience is the source.

### 5.2 When editing escalates beyond light touch

A heavier hand from the editing skill (or from Estef directly) is justified when:

- The piece combines multiple unrelated topics that should have been separate articles. Example pattern: an article that fused three different topics and included a prediction section about a fourth, with sections speaking to entirely different audiences. The fix is to cut everything that doesn't belong to the central story, even if it leaves gaps.
- The article addresses the topic from the wrong leadership angle (e.g., organizational when the publication needs technological, or vice versa). The fix is to request complementary information from the author to add the missing perspective.
- Personal experience is missing entirely despite earlier feedback. The fix is to send questions back asking for specific highlights — challenges, decisions, failures, observations — and integrate them.

The bar for radical editing is high. Most fellows' articles get the light pass. When radical editing is needed, the structural issue should be named clearly and specific cuts or additions proposed — not rewriting in editorial voice.

The wizard's role across both light and heavy editing scenarios is to flag when these escalation conditions appear to be present (so the editing skill or Estef knows to engage them) and to verify the resulting edit hit the relevant criteria.

### 5.3 What the editorial team owns vs. what the fellow owns

**Fellow owns:** the topic, the structure (number and order of sections), the byline, the central argument, the personal experience, final approval of the draft.

**Editorial team produces (via skills + Estef's judgment):**
- Editing of the draft itself (via `fellows-article-editing` skill)
- The dek (introductory standfirst)
- Graphic asset suggestions (via `graphic-assets-suggestions` skill)
- AEO and redundancy passes (within the editing skill)
- Stat verification and link insertion

Quote callouts during editing should be drawn only from text genuinely authored by the fellow. Editorial paraphrases or summaries do not qualify as pull-quotes.

## 6. Structure and Length

**Length:** 1,500–2,800 words.

**Section logic:**
- One main title
- A dek (produced by editorial during the edit pass)
- 3–8 body sections — the count and order are the fellow's call
- A closing statement

The structure is more permissive than PR op-eds because fellows' articles serve a different job. They are deeper expert dives, not opinionated commentary, and the section count flexes to match the complexity of what the fellow is unpacking.

**Headings:** Title case for H2s and H3s.

---

## 7. Voice

There is no centralized voice profile for fellows. Each fellow has their own voice and the editorial mandate is to preserve it.

What the wizard should still apply:

- The universal hard editorial rules (§5.1)
- The humanizing pass for AI tics (§5.1)
- A check that personality traits in the writing are kept, not flattened

What the wizard should not do:

- Apply Nacho's voice profile, Justice's voice prompt, or any other internal voice guide to a fellow's draft
- Smooth idiosyncratic phrasing unless it actively harms clarity
- Insert BairesDev-specific framing or vocabulary that the fellow didn't use

---

## 8. Approval and Publication

**Approval:** The fellow approves the final draft. There is no separate PR or media-team approval gate for this format.

**Distribution:** BairesDev's social media team owns distribution. The fellow is also expected to promote the article on their own channels. Neither responsibility carries an editorial implication — title shareability, pull-quote density, and similar concerns are not editorial decisions for this format.

---

## 9. Ready-to-Publish Verification (Wizard Checklist)

Before publication, the wizard verifies the following criteria are met. The wizard does not execute the work — the editing skill, the graphic-assets-suggestions skill, and Estef do. The wizard confirms each criterion has been satisfied:

1. The topic passed audience-type and challenge-clarity fit (wizard verifies)
2. Personal experience is integrated meaningfully (challenges, decisions, observations, failures, lessons)
3. The piece holds to a single central story; tangents have been cut
4. The audience is senior technology decision-makers throughout, with no drift
5. Universal hard editorial rules are applied
6. The humanizing pass has been run; AI tics are removed
7. AEO and redundancy passes are complete
8. Every external stat has a verified source linked; experience-grounded claims pass without sourcing
9. Dek is written; graphic asset suggestions are produced via the dedicated skill
10. The fellow has approved the final draft

If any criterion fails verification, the wizard surfaces the gap so the relevant skill or Estef can address it before publication.

## 10. Open Items and Flags

Items surfaced during this draft that need resolution as the other documents come together:

- **Fellows' content as source asset.** Published fellows' articles should be indexed in the Source Asset Index (Document 5) — not as past output, but as a feed of patterns, framings, and insights the wizard can mine when proactively flagging angles for PR op-eds and AI Hub articles. The indexing should capture: central thesis, key data points the fellow introduced, topic territory, and any framings the fellow named that could travel to other formats.
- **Anti-evergreen principle from the PR op-eds rulebook may apply here in modified form.** Fellows' articles can carry more durable framings than PR op-eds (a strong "what makes a high-performing engineering team" piece can rest partly on durable principles), but a fully evergreen fellows' piece — no time-anchored shift, no "why now" — is still weak content for the blog. Worth confirming during the cross-format sweep whether the same angle-vs-principles distinction applies.
- **AI tic library.** The named tics ("after the fact," "the organizations who win are the ones…") plus the universal hard rules form a growing list. Worth consolidating this into a cross-format "AI tics to strip" list — likely as part of a shared editorial principles doc or as an annex to the rulebook.
- **Skill integration.** The wizard should defer to the existing fellows-article-editing skill and graphic-assets-suggestions skill for execution detail. The rulebook tells the wizard *what* the format requires; the skills tell it *how* to execute. Worth noting this division of labor explicitly so the wizard doesn't try to reinvent skill logic.
